I didn't want to buy a plotter and a box of instruments. I wanted to build my own: a Raspberry Pi with OpenPlotter, a home-made autopilot from an old Autohelm, a wind instrument on a mount I cast right on the mast, and in the end a "NavPod" with real buttons. It took four years, and it's still growing. Engineers never finish; they just release the next version.

## A screen in the cockpit

In May 2023 I took out a leaking port to make room for a 15-inch, 600–800 nit touchscreen. It works next to the main nav station; a second, 7-inch, 1,200 nit screen will go on a nav pod, maybe with another Raspberry Pi. The port wasn't watertight at all: when I took out the screws, it just fell out of the hole. The cabin wall isn't flat, so to mount a screen I made a flat surface on the cabin side with thickened epoxy, pressed with a 3/4-inch MDF plate wrapped in freezer paper. A couple of wrinkles, but they shouldn't matter. The hole is closed with PVC tape for now. The inner rectangle is the display frame; the opening will grow about 2.5 inches. I don't expect structural problems.

## Planning the autopilot

In September 2022 I measured the steering for an autopilot.

![Notebook sketch of the steering geometry](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgxDlDj-Xwago4lZB4bNO6tHz8TOoCL0o-Q45b1clI8uwl6jLLh5KBAKTUyxdMWRXCkVfMm8ezGkapJxZB1xO5FXiYAaghf5WOEPpQz7DrQXl_k6oh3BpbzoGt8KVohUZURUHT891cxMekzOrZOku4dRBNbUpmkr20YrrMdi3i0KTo5enRm_2Pa-s8Pvw/s1600/IMG_3771.HEIC)

Diary: [10 Sep 2022](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2022/09/days-47-910-new-wiring-part-1-cockpit.html)

In February 2023 I did a lot of calculations. The final plan: close to a wheel drive, but "on steroids". The Irwin 34 steering: a wheel about 20 inches across, 2.5 turns lock to lock, so 900°. The rudder quadrant turns 80°. So the ratio is 900:80, about 11:1. Say 20 lb is the most you'd reasonably put on the wheel. On a 12-inch quadrant arm at 11:1 that's 2,640 lb-in, or 300 N·m.

I wanted a 300 mm wheel on the rudder stock, driven by a timing belt. The force at 300 mm is 300 N·m / 0.15 m = 2,000 N, the starting point for picking a belt. I chose an 18 mm HTD belt with fiberglass cords: 130 N/mm x 18 mm = 2,340 N, 17% spare. The pulleys: a 300 mm wheel and a 37 mm, 24-tooth sprocket, for 8.1:1.

## End switches from a home alarm

In July 2024, sailing with the autopilot, it twice drove past the rudder's travel, and the steering arm slipped on the rudder shaft. I had left the key out of the shaft on purpose, so nothing breaks if the autopilot goes crazy without end switches. I'll cut a keyway when I'm 100% sure of it. For now, slipping is better than fighting.

For end switches I first planned industrial heavy-duty ones, and even bought them. But I ended up with simple magnetic switches from home security systems. Why? They're fully sealed, so weather can't get to them. They have no moving parts touching anything, so nothing gets stuck, and they're reliable by design. And they're dirt cheap and easy to find anywhere on the planet.

The downsides, and what I did about them. They're not made to mount on metal, so I made mounting plates for the steering cross member. They're fragile, so they sit where nothing can hit them. And magnets can bother a compass, so I tested: no deviation at all on the pedestal compass.

A few things I still don't love. Each sensor bracket is held by one screw; I put super glue on the thread and under the bracket so it can't loosen, though maybe Loctite would be enough. The wires have no outer jacket, but they're zip-tied to the cross member, and they're just sensor wires, current-limited by the motor controller, so there's no fire risk. And the bolt from the steering arm to the linear drive is nut-up: if the nut loosens, the bolt could drop out. I should drill it for a pin, or at least Loctite it.

The box is IP68, with IP68 connectors, and holds the most sensitive parts: my baseboard, the Raspberry Pi and the HDMI splitter. The baseboard is like a big HAT, except that the Pi mounts on it instead of the other way around. It protects the Pi's interfaces from the marine environment and from mild static discharge. A direct lightning hit you can't really protect against; it's cheaper to keep a spare set of electronics in a Faraday box.

Inside the box:

- Raspberry Pi 5, 8 GB, mechanically secured.
- Two 4-port USB 2.0 hubs, 8 well-protected ports with 2 A, from two Pi ports.
- A direct RJ45 port for the Maiana AIS.
- IMU, and the PyPilot weather sensor with a barometric sensor and a direct connection for the Davis anemometer.
- Two galvanically isolated NMEA 0183 ports.
- A port for the PyPilot motor controller.
- Pi power control.
- A remote keyboard interface for controlling PyPilot (software not ready yet).
- Two USB power ports.
- The HDMI splitter copying the nav desk screen to the cockpit.

## The NavPod idea

At my desk in April 2025 I started on a "navpod": an OpenPlotter dashboard on the pedestal guard, shaped like a navpod, with buttons instead of touch. For me, buttons are much easier in the cockpit. A script will switch apps to the front and back, and arrow buttons can work as a mouse if I need something "non-standard", but mostly it'll be a dashboard. The modes:

- Map (an OpenCPN preset).
- VNC, a copy of the main nav screen.
- Sailing dashboard: big wind instrument, SOG, STW, day log, depth, position, COG.
- Engine dashboard: small wind instrument, tachometer, coolant out temperature, oil pressure, the other temperatures (coolant in, exhaust, gearbox, shaft packing, raw water after the exchanger), and SOG, STW, log, depth, position, COG.
- Autopilot controls and anchor/windlass control with a chain counter, as pop-up overlays.
- The masthead camera.

How the buttons work. - Take 10x the radar's weight both down and sideways without damage. Say 100 kg down plus 100 kg sideways. I think the radar would come apart before 200 kg got to the mount bolts.

- Aluminum, like the mast. Minimum fasteners.

## The NavPod is finished

May 2026: a weekend of NavPod software. It's a second Raspberry Pi head unit at the helm, with a 7-inch, 2,000-nit display, adjustable from 0.005% to 100%, talking to the main computer's Signal K, PyPilot and other apps. It has 26 buttons and mouse simulation. The left panel: power, a dedicated MOB button (it saves the position, opens OpenCPN and drops a MOB mark), AIS TX on/off, three app buttons, and the autopilot: mode (compass, wind, GPS, true wind, nav), AUTO/STBY, ±1°, ±10° and tack. The right panel: mode buttons (keyboard, numpad, mouse, lock), arrows with OK, and Enter, Esc and Tab.

The sailing screen: a wind/compass instrument with apparent wind (orange arrow), true wind (blue marker), set and drift (green teardrop), heading, the autopilot mode, the rudder position on a hull outline, and a red dot toward the next waypoint. The side panels are fully configurable: on the left SOG, STW, depth, water temperature (:-)) and rudder; on the right the time on each tack, and the next waypoint's direction, distance and speed. It will have sailing and motoring modes, and maybe day and night, but first I want to see it in bright sun.

## Small fixes

In August 2026 the wind sensor showed nothing on the dashboards. A quick check: the small sacrificial resistor on the sensor board's power was blown. Probably a discharge to the mast, or lightning close enough to do that little damage. A soldering iron and a piece of wire fixed it.

Diary: [9 Aug 2026](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2026/08/day-274-275-sailing-with-family-small.html)

In September I added one more GPS, 10 Hz, mainly for the autopilot's GPS mode. A faster GPS lets the autopilot work more precisely. Nothing fancy, a standard u-blox 10 module, but in a custom outdoor housing for the back rail. Now the 10 Hz GPS feeds PyPilot, and the old 1 Hz one stays connected to the Pi as a spare, not in use. Version 4.something of the whole system, and counting.
