The galley is where a boat becomes a home. When I bought Vira, it looked more or less OK, until you lifted anything up. Then everything was rotten. In the end I rebuilt it all: the base, the countertop, the propane, the cabinets, a drawer and even a gimbaled microwave.

## Rotten from the start

On Day 3 I cleaned the rotten plywood out of the galley wall cabinet and kept the frame as a template; that's in the Beginning story. In September 2022 the galley countertop came out. Nothing to cry about: it was rotten trash, and I knew that from the first moment.

## A new galley base

In May 2023 I decided to redo the front of the galley, with a new drawer frame and shelves on the left. Everything had been mounted very loosely. I took the walls out and put the three back panels in again, glued, with new screws. The plan: new front panels and new drawers. The original drawers were tiny and not practical at all.

Here it is before the demolition, and then with the walls out. Prototyping the new front; a laser helps a lot. Cables organized. I turned one wall to make room for the drawers.

The prep was done at home: the sink and storage openings routed on the CNC. Exact size for the sink, 12x12 inches for storage, and 12x16 for the refrigerator lid. The lids will need glued frames around them so they slide in snug. Then both pieces went to the boat, and strangely, every opening was in the right place! My biggest worry was the sink hitting the new drawers. Nope, all good.

I routed grooves so the wall cabinets sit tight on the countertop without trim, although I'll probably need some trim around the door and where it meets the next panel anyway. I glassed over some exposed foam in the bottom of the aft wall cabinet, in case of a leak. Yes, the epoxy is red: my fast hardener sat in its can too long. West System says it doesn't hurt the cured strength, so it'll be red until I use it up. And I cut out the gimbaled stove opening and made templates for joining the front and back countertop pieces.

After the first coat of primer I saw the problem: plywood REALLY needs to be sealed before you paint it. So I sanded off that first coat and put on three coats of wood sealer, so the grain almost doesn't show. A lot of time, but after three coats it looks very impressive.

I felt like a squirrel in a wheel of priming and sanding. Five coats of primer in total. Sounds crazy? It is. First, Pre-Kote (the lesson: seal plywood first if you're going to paint it), and I sanded almost all of that off. Second and third, Interlux wood sealer, with sanding between. Sand again. Fourth and fifth, Pre-Kote again, sanding between. At 6 hours minimum drying per coat, that's a lot of time. And all of it in a Texas summer, 40 °C outside. The AC ran 100% of the time and kept 26–28 °C inside: nice for relaxing, a bit sweaty for working.

Now it's nice and smooth, with no wood grain. I'm still practicing roll-and-tip; I think my brush isn't soft enough. The final paint will be in October.

## A drawer on roller-blade wheels

Every Irwin Citation 34 owner knows this place: a strange, hard-to-use cavity under the starboard cockpit seat, opened from the galley. And there's nothing to stop things from rolling out of it right onto the stove. So I made a drawer. It came out pretty big, about 20 by 16 inches.

There are no straight walls to mount rails on, so I had to invent something. The drawer rides on roller-blade wheels that run on the countertop, and full-extension stainless rails stop it from moving sideways. It needs to come out about 1.5 times its length to really reach inside, so I joined two rails together to keep it straight.

## A gimbaled microwave

My gimbaled stove came without an oven, so there was no counterweight to keep it level. I don't really need an oven, at least for now, so I put a microwave under the burners.
