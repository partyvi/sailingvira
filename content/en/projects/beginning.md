Every project boat story starts the same way: "I wasn't really looking for a project boat..." Mine too.

## The project boat found me

November 2021. We had been looking at boats around $40K on the coast, and we figured there was no way to get something within three hours' drive. Then I found an Irwin 34 on Facebook Marketplace, on Lake Travis, for $5K, thirty minutes from home.

Long story short: project boat.

![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/a/AVvXsEiyQA0qbz237Z-QhU0nteMv3hrrlyd5tV-ntYI7hfSgi5_EApb1mdV-_hpwvK0dUpd7DOVl-M_jHydkQh7imqrbXDqsnGq0Qka5sOjYnpb_XrT_7zhJznBQ5yWgYYediGEnB6qH4gg6OIGiSY9gFjvKMk0IMYRaISjfOBq8d3orJIOkBXCzqpeVGOEaWw=s1600)

Diary: [26 Nov 2021](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2021/11/project-boat-did-found-me.html)

An Irwin Citation 34 from 1978. A big project. Not total trash, but she needed a lot of love. People had been living aboard, and the cockpit was their backyard. An ugly RV-style AC sat on top, blocking the whole view from the cockpit, so you steered like a WWII pilot, looking only to the sides.

Then I took off the wall behind the microwave. Looking aft over the ice chest: the bulkhead between the cockpit lockers and the cabin was rotted out, and there was a hole into the ice chest through the countertop. Surprise, no insulation on top of the ice chest. Two things let all that water in: the stanchion bases (every one badly made and leaking) and the deck drains.

The main cabin looked tired. You could see water had stood on the floor for a while, so every floor panel had to go. The headliner was old and smelly. All the side panels were rotting, at different stages. The starboard bulkhead had been replaced, but the chainplates were leaking and it was getting wet again. Later I found it was teak veneer on particle board, over 1/2 inch of plywood of unknown quality.

## The list

Before buying, I wrote down what I knew. The short version:

- Lots of water damage, the interior after two years of liveaboards.
- Port lower front chainplate anchor rotten.
- Stanchions leaking like hell. Ports old and leaking, one fixed window cracked.
- Deck paint 40 years old, but maintainable.
- Furler needs service. Main sail really dirty, lots of green.
- Under water: cracks around the lower rudder bracket and the cutlass bearing strut.
- Soft engine hatch with no seal.
- Broken pulpit, no place for an anchor, but three anchors on board.
- Steering block on two mounting points. Engine control mounted wobbly, on plywood, with loose screws.
- All shrouds tightened to the end. Lifelines really old.
- Fuel tank sealed over the top. The owner swore he cleaned it and sealed it with very good adhesive... we'll see. Level glass cracked.
- Broken nav light glass, one spreader light dead.
- Known dismasting history, the mast replaced with one from an Irwin 30.
- Starboard bulkhead replaced but not glassed to the hull. Port bulkhead delaminated.
- A crack across the centerboard.
- Lots of trash in the lockers, maybe something useful in there.
- Companionway doors need rebuilding. A strange drain hole in the anchor well going inside. Cabin sole water-damaged.

## What I think of the Irwin 34

After almost four years with her, here's what I like about the original design:

- The price.
- Classic lines.
- Easy access to the engine.
- Encapsulated keel, low 4.5-foot draft.
- A roomy main cabin, 11.25-foot beam.
- U-shaped galley and a real nav desk.
- Solid fiberglass hull, no core.
- "Built-in" dorade boxes, at least on my boat.
- She sails pretty well.
- The thru-hulls sit in a closed locker, so in theory a failure floods only 5–10 cubic feet.
- An interesting mast: the spreader plate is welded in and splits the mast into upper and lower sections.
- The toilet sits fore and aft, so you can do your thing on both tacks.

And what I don't like:

- The stanchions. Sturdy idea, questionable execution. A vertical pipe through the deck takes side loads and cracks the thin sealant. The pipes are hollow and open at the top and at the lifeline holes, so they collect water. They're supposed to sit in glassed-in wood blocks, but on my boat many didn't.
- The bulkheads sit in the molded liner with no glue or sealant. Shower water collects there and soaks into the plywood, so the bottoms of all the bulkheads rotted.
- The compression post sits on the hanging cabin sole liner, not on the keel. The factory tried to fill the gap with a blob of epoxy and sand, but missed by about 4 inches. So the liner, never meant to carry the mast, cracks.
- A chain locker too small for a 34-footer, only 12–15 inches deep, and the hatch has no latch.
- No backing plates on the chainplates, especially forestay and backstay.
- The built-in fuel tank. Sealing a 10-foot plywood lid to a fiberglass tank that flexes with the hull is not realistic.
- Deck drains that are just 1-inch tubes stuck in holes with sealant.
- The deck-to-hull joint: OK in concept, bad in execution. Self-tapping screws into a small wood strip glassed to the hull edge, covered with an aluminum angle, also screwed.
- Cockpit drains joined into one thru-hull, with four 90° bends and about 10 feet of hose. Even worse, sink drain hose used for cockpit drains.
- Engine mounts on softwood blocks with lag bolts, taking both thrust and vibration.
- A centerboard axle glassed in with no side bearings or wear plates. It grinds the sides and gets sloppy.
- No flat spot on the keel forward of the centerboard to set the boat on blocks.
