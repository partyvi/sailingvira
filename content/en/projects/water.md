## The day tank disaster

Earlier I had filled the tank and a week later all the water was in the bilge. The tank is built into the boat, so I suspected a hose. To find it I had to close the gate valve on the tank outlet. It was stuck, so I took it out, freed it and cleaned it. Not too hard.

I closed it, filled the tank... and saw a nice stream running out of the bottom of the tank. So, I had to open it up.

And then several things pushed me to spend real money on it. The lid was just painted plywood, and it had rotted, of course. The inside of the tank had lots of pinholes with brown stuff in them. Nobody knows what paint is in there, and it's definitely not for drinking water. And there was a crack along the very bottom corner. Conclusion: rebuild the tank with materials made for drinking water.

I went shopping for an FDA-approved potable water tank coating. Most of them come only in barrels. I found a few in realistic sizes:

- Jotun Tankguard DW, in 40-gallon containers.
- LiquaTile 1172, $278 for 3 quarts.
- Belzona 5812 DW, $575 for 3.5 liters (one 10 mil coat, up to 140 sq ft).
- Belzona 5892, $850 for 4 liters, for hot environments.

TotalBoat suggested their polyester finishing resin, but it's not certified for drinking water. After long thinking I picked LiquaTile 1172.

I sanded the whole tank to get rid of flaking paint and open the crack, and found a pretty big void in the bottom, just left of center, with a crack across the whole bottom. To fix the leak I glassed the whole bottom: thickened resin first, then two layers of 1708 on the bottom corner, wet on wet, each a little offset so there's no step. That's me and the tank.

My glassing table is covered with butcher paper, so the resin doesn't ruin it and the wet glass doesn't stick.

## The front tank, and the mysterious hole

Through the inspection port of the front tank I could see osmosis pox and brown traces. Not good for drinking water. The lid screws were black drywall screws, rusted like hell, most of them in at 45°. When I tried to undo them they snapped at their rusty necks. So I cut the lid into pieces to get it off. Looks like somebody used Liquid Nails instead of sealant.

And the most mysterious thing: a 6 mm hole drilled in the middle of the tank bottom. I didn't believe my eyes. I thought it was chipped gelcoat, so I pushed a screw in it, and it went right in.

Osmosis pox all over the inside. I'm not worried about strength, but about chemicals getting into the water, and whether the surface could hold a two-part coating. Around the frame, it looked like three different attempts to mount the lid, with no thought that every hole in the frame is a future leak. A lid of exterior plywood, of course. And the cherry on top: the drilled hole. Who? Why? For what? More questions than ideas.

I sanded and filled all the pox, the frame holes and the bottom hole, glassed over them, and added tape on the bottom edges. Then two coats of LiquaTile 1172 inside. It's the only product certified for drinking water that I could buy in a sane amount, and three quarts was exactly enough for two coats. It's made for concrete and steel, but the maker confirmed it works on fiberglass. It's really hard to work with, very thick, and no thinners allowed. It cures to a nice gloss, except where I put it on too thick and it ran. Not a cosmetic surface, so who cares.

The only compromise was butyl tape on the lid. My plan: fill the tank completely with city water, freeze a sample while filling, wait 2–3 weeks, take a sample from the tank, and send both to a lab.

## PEX, and four days of zip-ties

"Don't try to make it better if it works!" Short story: my boat is 40 years old, and all the hoses were original, hard and starting to crack. I didn't want to wait for them to burst, so I replaced the old PVC hose with PEX-A.

Why PEX-A? The crimping is simple. You can expand the pipe somewhere comfortable and then install it in a hard place. The fittings are cheaper than SharkBite, there are no metal rings like PEX-B, so no corrosion, and the expander tool isn't expensive.

![New PEX tubing laid out on the dock](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhh1BwrDNv71rIgJCUDFf4oF8DRoQrHq1J15Wgyvy5eCzYWWTXgZ3mnYXKTRaecsUY3_MdJv2sF69_nvZWcrZ1Vv7fBTLA2TSCEgAKs3STNdiR99CJrBC4frOq3Vza49yk4BI173Wt5ny7D8it5wYPMOkdUaicljI-WRlViLzkYPH8x7J5k__vdrIo_KA/s1600/IMG_4496.HEIC)

Diary: [29 Dec 2022](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2022/12/day-70-73-261722-291722-fresh-water.html)

Easy to say. In my head it was simple: take off the clamps, tape the new PEX to the old hose, and pull it through using the old hose as a guide. A couple of hours, right?

In reality: four days. Why? All the hoses are tied with screw-mount zip-ties between the hull and the liner, in places you can't reach. I couldn't pull them, so I looked with a borescope and saw the zip-ties. Why did Irwin do that? Probably all the plumbing was done on the liner before it went into the hull, and the zip-ties held the hoses in place during assembly. Nobody thought anyone would ever replace the water hoses.

- Trap 1: the hose from the front tank to the port tank, tied 5 inches under the toilet. From the front cabin locker my hand was too short and too big. I had to cut a 2x2-inch hole in the floor next to the toilet. Crazy, I know, but I don't think 40-year-old heater hose is good for drinking water.
- Trap 2: behind the starboard settee, from the head to the galley, tied in three places with no access. There's a "bulkhead" in the middle of the settee the hoses go through, but on my boat there's about an inch gap between it and the hull. So I cut the old hoses, pushed them out of the original holes under the nav desk, then pushed and fished the PEX through to the head.
- Trap 3: from the galley to the water heater in the engine room. The hoses are foamed in under the ice chest, in some high-density stuff. The best way was a new path between the ice chest and the engine room: enlarge the exit hole, lay the new pipes over the old hoses and leave the old ones there. Luckily, on my boat it was mostly soft foam I could dig through with a stick, and only 3–4 inches of the hard foam near the aft corner I had to drill.

## The butyl lid leaks

August 2025, about two years after rebuilding the tanks, I finally started using them, and right away found the front tank lid leaking into the V-berth.

Funny thing, it came down to the sealant. The small port tank had silicone, and on the big front tank I'd tried butyl. The butyl wasn't the problem. In one corner the gap between the tank lip and the lid was too wide, so the tape never got squeezed. That left an open path, and of course, a leak.

To find it I hooked the shop vac up in blow mode to pressurize the tank and sprayed soapy water on the seams. The bubbles showed it right away.

"Just reseal it" sounds simple, but first the lid has to come off. That was a real fight. It was cut so snug there was no room to get anything under it, and butyl doesn't let go easily. After scratching at the seam a few times, I came up with a trick. I took out all the screws and let the shop vac blow into the tank. It only makes maybe 3–5 psi, but the lid is about 20 by 30 inches, around 600 square inches. Even at the low end that's a couple hundred pounds pushing up. Sure enough, it popped the lid up a little. Then I could get screwdrivers into the gap and work around until it came free.

![Front tank lid with butyl tape, sensor wire and inspection port](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhR_7dpHDSDcUcr9mbk7gJQbtmwzxFJhzSAcBmp0nsh2SdyfAsJIreqxSWn-NEizvW3CHrH_rk0Kp_IjMF6b8oLzOkwcJAdMv-CiK2tzajIfFnbUZ7-c4soX8eHrJAKTNsOdGxnde2d3FVaYXZQ53bIXtOhP6abFvaQ_CkQ-wL-Sgkx5rr-IKLwSsnmOt_G/s1600/IMG_1154.HEIC)

Diary: [17 Aug 2025](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2025/08/day-222-re-sealing-front-water-tank.html)

Cleaning off the butyl was another problem. It really only comes off mechanically, with a knife blade. And butyl sticks to HDPE like crazy. Cleaning the lid took an hour by itself, and another hour for the tank lip. But I found a trick: spray soapy water while you scrape. Without it, the little bits of butyl kept sticking back to the surface I'd just cleaned. With a spray bottle of Dawn, hand soap and water, it stopped sticking and went twice as fast and much cleaner. A final wipe with acetone, which doesn't dissolve butyl but softens it a bit.

After about two hours of cleaning I ran a nice thick bead of silicone and carefully dropped the lid back on. I tightened the screws only until the sealant squeezed into the seam, so the silicone works like a gasket. A week later, when it had cured a bit, I tightened them down to really compress it.

## Bleach, a fill controller and a real inlet

March 2026, in Kemah. The water heater went back in with a new riveted aluminum bracket on its front ear. The original mount meant removing the electrical box every time to get to the screws. The bracket is shaped so the screws take only side load, not pulling. And an IP68 power connector. I also pulled the empty black water tank and checked the inlet flange: 1.5 inches.

Diary: [9 Mar 2026](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2026/03/day-256-257-fixing-wind-instrument.html)

June 2026 I noticed a fishy smell from the fresh water tanks, so it was time for a proper sanitizing. I chlorinated the whole system: 300 ml of bleach in about 80 gallons of water, flushed through all the lines and left for about 20 hours. Then I drained and refilled the tanks with city water three times.

Filling the tanks is never fun. I had designed a water fill controller a while ago but was too lazy to install it. Now it's in. It opens a solenoid valve in the fill line at power-up, an inductive sensor sees when the tank is full, and it shuts the water off. While filling it beeps briefly every few seconds so you don't forget the water is running. When it's full it switches to a much more annoying half-second beep every second, to remind you to disconnect the dock hose.

Why electronics instead of a simple float valve? First, I can design and build electronics myself, make them reliable and keep the cost down. Second, a float valve inside the tank means opening the tank when it fails, and some fiddling so it fills completely without overflowing. An outside float valve needs extra plumbing to sense the level, and on a sailboat there's always a chance of water finding its way into the bilge when you heel. With the electronics everything I'd service stays reachable (at least for me), the tank fills almost to the top, and I get automatic shutoff plus a reminder. I also added a fuse panel in the HVAC compartment.
