My Yanmar 2QM20 is in very good shape. It probably never saw salt water, and I'd like it to work for me forever. So I converted it to fresh water cooling, without original 2QM20F parts: off-the-shelf parts, a few I made myself, and one heat exchanger from Mr. Cool Marine. The whole conversion, as I wrote it along the way, is in my original diary article: [Engine - Fresh water cooling](https://diary.sailingvira.com/p/engine-fresh-water-cooling-in-progress.html).

## Fresh water cooling

The engine is in very good shape. It probably never saw salt water, and I'd like it to work for me forever. I also want hot water on board, ideally with the option to heat it from the engine coolant. Yes, the system gets more complex, but I hope the benefits win.

First I checked the water passages in the head and block: no scale or corrosion. I DID NOT use original Yanmar 2QM20F parts. They're VERY hard to find, so prohibitively expensive, and almost impossible to fix quickly if something fails. The only exception is the heat exchanger, made by Mr. Cool Marine.

Off-the-shelf parts:

- A 2QM20 heat exchanger from Mr. Cool, modified for 1-inch fresh water connections: the top one turned a different way, and a 90° on the bottom one.
- A Yanmar 129001-42005 fresh water pump with a 121450-42350 pulley.
- A 3-way inline thermostat, 709200406, from a Defender ATV, supposed to be 82–85 °C.
- The overheat switch replaced with one from a 2GM20F, Yanmar 127610-91350, 95 °C.
- A new 1/2 x 40-inch V-belt, D&D AX38. 40 inches works, but 42–43 would be much easier to fit. Now I have to pry it on while turning the engine, or take the pump pulley off.
- BSPT 3/4 to 1-inch hose barbs instead of the original water ports. The original 5/8 hoses are too small and can cause hot spots and too much thermal stress on the block and head.
- 1-inch heater hose with 90° elbows on the fresh water side.

Custom parts:

- A flat front head cover from 12 mm steel, using the original as a template. The alarm sensor hole goes where there's room on the pump plate.
- A water pump plate from 12 mm steel, made from the Lexan template I used to tune the pump position. The pump's outlet points at the block's water inlet, and one stud is shared with the head cover. The V-belt clears the pump body by about 10 mm, so I'll watch the belt tension. Everything painted except under the pump, with an opening for the alarm sensor, mounted on the three studs that hold the head cover.
- An alternator tensioner bar from 6 mm steel. The SendCutSend part was straight, but it hit a bolt on the pump, so I heated it red hot and forged it, first flat, then vertical to line up with the alternator.
- A heat exchanger bracket from 3/16 steel, welded, next to the exhaust manifold, using the M6 points that held the fuel filter and a bolt from the raw water thermostat housing.
- A fuel filter bracket, also 3/16 welded, moving the filter 4 inches lower to make room, held by longer exhaust manifold bolts with washers.
- A coolant "manifold" from 3/4 copper pipe joining the heat exchanger, the thermostat and the pump inlet.

## Back on the boat

Then the heat exchanger. On the engine its inlet had no access, so I mounted it separately: above the wet muffler, as close to the engine as possible, level, and high enough that air runs out naturally. Slightly longer hoses, but much better access. It took two hours to get it tight, with about 2 inches between it and the wet exhaust hose. Finding a place for the coolant expansion tank was a little struggle; for now it's on the wall to the right.

So far, about 15 hours since the fresh water conversion. Some people said it's good, some said it would kill the engine instantly. Zero issues so far. I've run it several times for an hour at cruising RPM, about 2,000. On February 13, 2026 we motored 5 hours nonstop at 2,000–2,200 RPM. The engine settled at about 76 °C coolant out and 70 °C going back into the block after the exchanger. No sign of overheating. The old Yanmar is doing just fine with its new life.
