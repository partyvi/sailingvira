August 2026: our first real weekend trip on Vira. Out of Kemah, across the ship channel, a night at anchor in Galveston, and back home. The day sails on the bay are in [Day sailing Galveston Bay](../galveston-bay-sailing/).

## Kemah to Galveston and back

August 2026, a real full weekend. This trip was:

- The longest weekend trip I've ever done on Vira.
- The closest she's been to REAL sea water.
- My first time in Galveston, and the first night away from the home marina.
- Sailing in the busiest commercial traffic I've ever seen, and using AIS for real, not for fun.
- The first try at a night without AC at 30 °C (but then I cheated at a slip).
- The first time crossing the ship channel in Galveston Bay, and the first cooking underway.
- The first absolutely sober sailing in a while.

Early Saturday morning in the marina; out of Kemah at about 10:20. Captain VicVic. Peaceful cruising toward Galveston in 5–6 knots, hiding in the genoa's shade. A motorsailer in the Galveston channel. Then beating hard as the wind came up.

Getting closer to Galveston, I really understood why I had installed the AIS transponder and how useful it is in tight traffic. Here we are on the MarineTraffic map; sadly I didn't screenshot our own screens. Being right in that traffic, with cargo ships, barges, ferries and dozens of smaller boats, was pretty intense. Not nearby, not watching it, but in it. An ADULT feeling.

The Coast Guard docks in Galveston. We officially anchored at the Teacup anchorage. A nice dinner after a long day, and a sunset on the hook.

When I had asked on a forum where to anchor in Galveston, they recommended Teacup, but also warned that there's a lot of traffic nearby and it's not exactly calm. OK, I'm not looking for an easy life, and it's one night. After dinner we saw just how much traffic there was. The wind held us almost exactly beam-on to the wakes, so every 5–10 minutes the boat rolled 15–20 degrees from side to side. It was not going to be a sweet-dreams night.

One guy on the forum who mentioned Teacup (though he didn't exactly recommend it) had also said I could use a free empty courtesy slip at the marina where he lives aboard, five minutes away by motor. So I messaged him. He answered almost immediately: "I'm on the docks in a yellow shirt. I'll point you to a slip where you can stay overnight." A very heartwarming moment.

We pulled the anchor and motored over in the last light. D Yami met us on the dock and helped us tie up, Vic thanked him, and he just quietly walked away. I invited him aboard for tea, rum, gin or cider, but apparently helping two strangers find a peaceful night was enough socializing for him, and the invitation went unanswered. He had also told us we could plug into shore power. So instead of rolling 15–20 degrees all night at anchor, we had a quiet dock, electricity, AC and a good night's sleep.

Sometimes the best piece of cruising gear is just a helpful person on the dock in a yellow shirt.

The next morning we didn't want to overuse the hospitality, or get "caught trespassing", so we left as early as we could. The plan was 6 AM, but after a good night with the AC I barely scraped myself out of bed at 7. A quick coffee, some breakfast, and off the dock ASAP. Symphony of the Seas had been docked about 1,000 feet from us that night; I took her picture as we passed. Goodbye, Galveston, city of big ships.

On the way back we followed the east side of the ship channel. Nice wind, sailing at 15° of heel. We met a boat from our marina on the way. And these bastards, lovebugs, found us in the middle of Galveston Bay, hundreds of them! They don't bite, they don't sting, but they're too annoying. And they fly in pairs, so it's always twice as many as you think. At 3 PM the wind died below 2 knots, so we started the engine and motored the last hour home.
