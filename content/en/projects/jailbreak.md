For four years Vira lived on Lake Travis. A 34-foot boat in a lake with no way out to big water. In the fall of 2025 I decided to fix that. I called it the jailbreak.

## The plan

October 2025. The plan: get her ready for the road. Mast down and tied on deck, tanks drained, everything she doesn't need taken off. Then truck her to a boatyard on the coast. I was thinking Rockport then. Put her on the hard for a few months and finally do everything I couldn't do in the water:

- replace the thru-hulls with composite ones
- fix the strut
- add a lightning ground
- check the rudder and fix it if needed
- replace the centerboard hinge pin, depending how bad it is
- repair whatever I find under water (I was sure there'd be something)
- prep and paint the topsides
- strip all the old bottom paint, barrier coat, new antifouling
- recoat the mast and boom
- new standing rigging
- rewire the mast with a conduit inside and all new wires
- radar, tricolor light, fix the masthead camera
- AIS

Then splash her and take her to a new home marina on Galveston Bay.

First the sails, sail pack, battens and cockpit cushions came off and went below. Boom off and tied down. Mast waiting to come down.

## An A-frame from 2x4s

I had to drop the mast myself. Inspired by a YouTube video, I built an A-frame from 2x4s. Each leg is four 10-foot boards, the middle two sandwiched between the end ones and bolted, so each leg is almost three boards long. The top should be about 3 feet above the mast's center of gravity, around 24 feet up. I cut the bottom board about 2 feet shorter and at an angle so it sits better on the deck, with the toe rail stopping it from sliding. With 1.5-foot overlaps each leg came to 25 feet.

I laid the legs on the deck and joined the tops with an eyebolt to hoist the mast from. Then I lifted the frame with the main halyard. Doing it alone was interesting: cranking the winch and walking the legs back into place at the same time. I drilled the toe rail to tie each leg down, then built the cross members and braces to make it stiff.

## The cursed day

Mast day was a bit cursed. My friend Eugene and I planned to meet at 7 AM, but he had to make a detour and came around 9. Two cool hours lost. Then I realized I'd forgotten the climbing gear and drove home and back. Another hour. I really missed those hours later: only 26 °C, but full Texas sun, and at least three times I was right at the edge of heatstroke.

Then we "dropped" the mast about two inches. I was so focused on where and how to land it that I let out all the rope in the lifting tackle and forgot to watch the end.

![Vira in her slip, last days on the lake](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiu7Bhn7nYbONzyeLhAkkMr8H-EYp4M8ESmmvcNdzkOG7c0PyKg1e1YQAysatqzKIII38CHkyg3hqOQpm3uPv9Ups-VJ_QCqKioOPBubAE4IeH2-tLoXJRdd04BNsIm0CSNUBhfjWoKPKTYW-ClIC6gCl52cetKafoF_WBA2Vmsd2WJUZtuouSfAU7wz-EV/s1600/IMG_1775.HEIC)

After that the A-frame had to come down. We took off the cross members, untied the legs and started lowering it forward. The idea was to walk the leg bottoms onto the dock and take them apart there. But I completely forgot about the spring lines holding the boat from moving forward. As soon as the legs went onto the dock, the boat slid forward 3–4 feet. The frame tilted about 30 degrees, one leg got trapped between the dock and a stanchion, and a 2x4 snapped. Then everything went like a slow-motion movie. The frame came down and landed softly, mostly on the lifelines. We were lucky: nothing broken, nobody hurt. No pictures, we were a bit busy.

## Jailbreak day

I had booked the transporter half a year before, for November 11. Of course nothing went as planned. We made it one day late, with a different trailer, a different truck and different people helping with the haul-out. But we did it.

Early morning in Austin. We met at the marina at 6:30 to motor to Austin Yacht Club, where she would come out. Disconnect shore power and go. Goodbye, Commanders Point Marina. It was a great home for four years, but we're moving on. Vira said goodbye too. Her last 30 minutes motoring on Lake Travis.

About 11 AM we were almost ready to go. Exactly when Rob was about to hook up, Fred, the trailer owner, shouted: "Not so fast! I need to change all six wheel bearings. ## Friday the 13th, a low bridge and fog

We left Hillman Boatyard in Dickinson at 5 PM, waiting for high tide so we'd have enough water in the channel. Destination: Kemah Boardwalk Marina.

Right at the start there was a 44-foot bridge, and our mast is about 46 feet. So we got creative. We heeled the boat: one guy out on the boom, another leaning hard over the side, and a barrel we tried to use for weight. We got about 10 degrees of heel. A camera at the masthead watched the clearance. It was tight, very tight, but we made it.

Then 5.8 nautical miles of Dickinson Bayou channel, following the markers in skinny water. Then 2.6 miles along the Galveston ship channel toward Redfish Island, staying clear of the big ships, 4.1 miles across to the Kemah channel, and 2.3 more to the marina.

The first part was nice in daylight. Around sunset fog started creeping in. Then thicker, and thicker, until we could barely see a boat length. We could only see the channel lights from about 50 feet. For two-thirds of the trip we went only on instruments, GPS and compass. Moving through total gray, trusting the plotter and listening for other boats. Luckily there was little traffic. Once we were lined up in the Kemah channel it got easy.

My brave crew: Viktor and Andriy. 10 PM, Friday the 13th, 2026. We did it.
