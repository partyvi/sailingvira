And then the first time under sail in Galveston Bay. The very first time. About 5 hours, 15 knots, choppy water, but a real joy to sail after all that work. Leaving Kemah, the taste of freedom, me and my son. The autopilot didn't work after all the reassembly. At the dock we found that the key had fallen out of the rudder stock keyway, so the arm just turned on the stock instead of steering the boat. So Viktor hand-steered almost the whole time. And we came back to the dock in one piece.

## Brochure forecasts

I planned a nice, gentle sail with my wife. The forecast confidently promised 5–8 knots, waves under a foot and partly cloudy skies. A brochure-perfect day. Reality, of course, delivered 10–15 knots and short, choppy 1–1.5 foot waves. Still OK, right up until it was time to go back. Then the drizzle started, and we had to go dead downwind. For the next hour we got the full education in downwind sailing: rolling, wobbling, and questioning our life choices. Needless to say, Maria was not happy.

There were some happy moments, though. Me and my happy wife, motoring out of the Kemah channel, my brave co-captain Vic, and the autopilot on duty. And Saturday night at a local restaurant.

May: two days, one for work and one for pleasure. Not many pictures, just my happy wife. The forecast said 0.7-foot seas and 5–10 knots. We sailed to Redfish Island, about 8 miles from Kemah. Beating into the wind, but not bad: she ran nicely with a little heel at 3–5 knots, and everyone was happy. We anchored with a couple of other boats and swam in the bay for the first time. The water was great.

As always, I used the chance to check under the boat. Task one: rope on the prop. When we motored away from the boatyard, I had caught the swim ladder line in the prop. Thank God it was old enough to break, or we'd have been in real trouble. But part of it had stayed there all this time. And yes, about 4 feet of rope were wrapped around the shaft and the prop. The bottom paint works GREAT. But the prop and shaft had small barnacles. Looks like I'll need to clean them every month.

Magic always has a price. While we relaxed at anchor, the wind came up to 15–20 knots and the waves built to about 2 feet. It's the bay, so the waves are short, and the motion gets uncomfortable fast. An hour and a half of rock-and-roll, even though we weren't dead downwind. On the other hand, a steady 7 knots, almost hull speed.

## The water under the floor

July 2026, Saturday morning. We hoisted the new sail and went out to sea-trial the new standing rigging. 15–20 knots. A great sail, though the weather never let us put up the full main, so most of the day was 5–6 knots with the first reef and just a "corner" of the jib.

At one point a stronger gust, maybe 20+, put the toe rail in the water. Nothing excessive, just a normal reaction to a decent gust. Five or ten minutes later I heard the bilge pump alarm. I jumped into the cabin and found 2–3 inches of water under the cabin sole. My boat has a very shallow bilge, so even 5–10 gallons looks dramatic.

First thought: "Houston, we have a problem." We weren't far from the marina, so my first reaction was "let's head back". Then curiosity took over. Where was all that water coming from? A quick taste test gave the answer: fresh water.

I knew right away. I had forgotten to close the fill valve between the main tank in the V-berth and the small day tank on the port side. The water in the front tank rose above the top of the vent line and simply poured out through the vent into the bilge.

Then the stress turned into laughing, and we had the rest of the day for sailing.
