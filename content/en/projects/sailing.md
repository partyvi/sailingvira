All this work is for one thing: to go sailing. Here are the days on the water, the people who came along, and the life around the boat: docks that move, a lake that dries up and comes back, storms, and finally Galveston Bay.

## Girls together

December 2021, Day 4. Nothing done on the boat. We just sailed Sea Sun, my Hunter 19, from West Beach Marina to Commanders Point Marina, to keep her next to the Irwin 34. By the way, the name is decided: the Irwin is Vira now. A bit over two hours, about 6–7 NM, 3–5 knots, a nice 5–20 knot wind, 10–13 °C and rain at the start. Thanks Eugene for the help. Now the girls are together!

Then the sailing day, with a big crew: me, my son, my father and Eugene. Lots of fun. Mostly learning this new boat. The wind kept changing speed, which was a bit annoying, but some tacks were really fun. I learned how to handle the big genoa and the relatively small main. She heels pretty easily to about 20 degrees, then holds it there. We tried beating with 100° tacks and it worked.

Some incidents. We had an "intentional" man overboard drill: dropped the sails and motored back to pick up the "MOB". And water came in from a broken PVC pipe that ran from the "chain locker" to the cockpit drain thru-hull. The open end of the broken pipe went under water when the boat heeled to port, so we got 5–6 gallons in the bilge. Very minor. I clamped a rubber hose between the thru-hull and the PVC pipe so it can't happen again.

## Docks that move

Late August: Vira went from the repair dock to a pocket slip, much easier for going sailing. That's the good news, and probably all of it. The slip is only about a foot wider than her beam, the corners have no protection, and it's the fourth dock from the lake, so every wake rocks the boat. I spent money on dock wheels. To fit 12-inch wheels I had to set the mounts flush with the dock, otherwise the boat couldn't get in and out. I started early, to get a couple of hours before the sun got high and the real Texas hell began.

## Swapping slips

After the marina turned the whole dock and Vira's transom ended up too close to the shore, the owner agreed I could swap slips with the boat across the walkway. We did it one evening after work, a good workout. The part I was nervous about went VERY easily: turning Vira in a tiny space, backing all the way out through the narrow water and back around to the office dock. I worried about prop walk, but it wasn't as bad as people said. Then we had to move the neighbor's boat into Vira's old spot by "walking" her along the docks with boat hooks. About 600 feet, and she didn't want to go straight. A couple of times we drifted too far and had to "fishtail" the rudder to get back. About an hour of fun and both boats were in their new slips.

## Brochure forecasts

I planned a nice, gentle sail with my wife. The forecast confidently promised 5–8 knots, waves under a foot and partly cloudy skies. A brochure-perfect day. Reality, of course, delivered 10–15 knots and short, choppy 1–1.5 foot waves. Still OK, right up until it was time to go back. Then the drizzle started and we had to go dead downwind. For the next hour we got the full education in downwind sailing: rolling, wobbling, and questioning our life choices. Needless to say, Maria was not happy.

Some happy moments, though. Me and my happy wife, motoring out of the Kemah channel, my brave co-captain Vic, and the autopilot on duty. And Saturday night at a local restaurant.

## The water under the floor

July 2026, Saturday morning. We hoisted the new sail and went out to sea-trial the new standing rigging. 15–20 knots. A great sail, though the weather never let us put up the full main, so most of the day was 5–6 knots with the first reef and just a "corner" of the jib.

At one point a stronger gust, maybe 20+, put the toe rail in the water. Nothing excessive, just a normal reaction to a decent gust. Five or ten minutes later I heard the bilge pump alarm. I jumped into the cabin and found 2–3 inches of water under the cabin sole. My boat has a very shallow bilge, so even 5–10 gallons looks dramatic.

First thought: "Houston, we have a problem." We weren't far from the marina, so my first reaction was "let's head back". Then curiosity took over. Where was all that water coming from? A quick taste test gave the answer: fresh water.

I knew right away. I'd forgotten to close the fill valve between the main tank in the V-berth and the small day tank on the port side. The water in the front tank rose above the top of the vent line and just poured out through the vent into the bilge.

Then the stress turned into laughing, and we had the rest of the day sailing.

When I asked on a forum where to anchor in Galveston, they recommended Teacup, but also warned there's a lot of traffic nearby and it's not exactly calm. OK, I'm not looking for an easy life, and it's one night. After dinner we saw just how much traffic there was. The wind held us almost exactly beam-on to the wakes, so every 5–10 minutes the boat rolled 15–20 degrees side to side. Not going to be a sweet-dreams night.

One guy on the forum who mentioned Teacup (though he didn't exactly recommend it) had also said I could use a free empty courtesy slip at the marina where he lives aboard, five minutes away by motor. So I messaged him. He answered almost immediately: "I'm on the docks in a yellow shirt. I'll point you to a slip where you can stay overnight." A very heartwarming moment.

We pulled the anchor and motored over in the last light. D Yami met us on the dock, helped us tie up, Vic thanked him, and he just quietly walked away. I invited him aboard for tea, rum, gin or cider, but apparently helping two strangers find a peaceful night was enough socializing for him, and the invitation went unanswered. He'd also told us we could plug into shore power. So instead of rolling 15–20 degrees all night at anchor, we had a quiet dock, electricity, AC and a good night's sleep.

Sometimes the best piece of cruising gear is just a helpful person on the dock in a yellow shirt.

Next morning we didn't want to overuse the hospitality, or get "caught trespassing", so we left as early as we could. The plan was 6 AM, but after a good night with the AC I barely scraped myself out of bed at 7. Quick coffee, some breakfast, and off the dock ASAP. Symphony of the Seas was docked about 1,000 feet from us that night; I took her picture as we passed. Goodbye, Galveston, city of big ships.

On the way back we followed the east side of the ship channel. Nice wind sailing, 15° of heel. We met a boat from our marina on the way. And these bastards found us in the middle of Galveston Bay, hundreds of them! They don't bite, they don't sting, but they're too annoying. At 3 PM the wind died below 2 knots, so we started the engine and motored the last hour home.
