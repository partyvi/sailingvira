On this boat there's one main rule: everything on the deck that I haven't touched yet is leaking. I learned it the hard way, one piece of hardware at a time. This is the story of the deck itself: soaked core, empty stanchion boxes, a hull-to-deck joint held together by hope, and a lot of epoxy.

## New core

I cut a new core from marine plywood, the same as the original. I sanded and degreased both sides, put polyester resin with a little chopped glass on the bottom skin, laid the plywood in, and loaded it with four bags of sand. I needed more, but that's all I had.

Two hours later, when the resin had gelled, a layer of thickened resin went on, and the old top skin went back on top, under fourteen bags of sand. I finished at 8:30 PM.

Then came glassing the gaps. Easy to say, but it was four hours of pretty nasty work.

The non-skid pattern was a problem. It's a nice pattern, but making it again on the repaired area isn't simple. So I made a mold from the existing deck: mold release, gelcoat, one layer of 1708. I only had finishing gelcoat with me, so I didn't wait for it to fully set and laminated as soon as it gelled. It didn't gel everywhere, flaked and mixed with the resin, so it got messy. Probably I'll make it again, but it doesn't look bad even now. Here's the original deck surface, and my home-made "negative".

## Stanchions: empty boxes

Next came the stanchions. On the starboard aft one I cut the old mount out. There was no wood left at all. The flange had a strange 3 mm step on one side. It looks like it was meant to overlap the toe rail, but the holes were drilled in the wrong place.

I made a new oak block, drilled an oversized hole for the pipe, filled it with structural putty and drilled it again, so only plastic touches the metal. Then I set it to the hull with the same putty and glassed it in: two layers of 24 oz woven roving and 1708 on top. The bottom overhang will be cut off.

Then all the old stanchion support boxes came out. All five we opened were empty, with the wood rotted away completely. Someone had put a thin layer of epoxy over the cracked gelcoat and broken glass around the screws, then 3M 5200 on top. The 5200 stuck to the deck pretty well, but not at all to the stainless. And all the stanchions are hollow, so any water that gets into a pipe goes straight to the wood. A good deal for future maintenance. My dad helped me with this one.

A week later the oak blocks went in from inside, with 1-inch holes drilled for the stanchion pipes, so only plastic touches them. I marked the screw spots, drilled them out to 10 mm and filled them with thickened epoxy, for 1/4-20 threads and 2-inch screws later. For now there's one layer of 3 oz glass over the stanchion holes, until I paint the deck. Then we glassed the blocks to the hull with two layers of 24 oz roving.

## The deck-to-hull nightmare

On the starboard side the hull-to-deck joint had no sealing at all and leaked in several places. The old sealant was dry and came out in pieces. So I decided to take the aluminum toe rail off and look.

Uff... it was hard work: a rubber mallet and the oscillating tool to break the 5200. And up close, the rail had never really been sealed. The sealant mostly didn't stick to the aluminum, and it covered maybe 10% of the area where it should be.

![Hull-to-deck joint opened up inside](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjhwllZ5HzEhGtueMROBsrezn6qtt1MDpq4cRN7bmX57BTMCXM77r83zDS8-kYluDzWFmVfrbYOMUVzZR5X2h3gr13fUrsiyNCFX32jzjFUA6scmMEBhvJzEAIm82P-a2BP5t-owX5gYh8TBMES_2RWZrwuhA51_DCwYgf8cumbCh07nMnSCNWXhMDCig/s1600/IMG_2742.HEIC)

What's under the rail? The previous owner had already "sealed" the joint: he cut a 5x5 mm groove and put some putty in it. That's it. With the hull flexing and almost no bonding area, it cracked and let water in.

I made a stupid mistake: I left masking tape under the laminate. Then I spent ages sanding out the edge and sanded through the gelcoat where the tape was. NEVER again.

Then the dry fit of the rail, and drilling the screw holes. I almost cried drilling holes in the thing I had just made waterproof. Then I "painted" it all with gelcoat. Gelcoat in that heat is really hard.

The next day I sanded the gelcoat off. To get it smooth I had to sand nearly all of it away. So: no fun, time and money for nothing. Lesson: at least three coats of gelcoat, probably 3–4, or don't bother.

## Stanchions done right

October 2022. Here's how the stanchions should be, from Irwin's own drawing. The problems with the design:

- The stanchions are hollow pipes, so water gets in easily and runs down to the wood block.
- The block has no drain, so water collects there, and you don't see it until it's completely rotten.
- The pipe is welded to the flange, but not all the way around, so there are gaps.
- The flange is held to the deck with short wood screws.

![Irwin drawing of the stanchion installation](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhV1fK7uXd-81dWyPDJITEFR5H85QWxtwpMsuX_TYA-U44Ml9xvv2AayLp_-k1O56dZiNRi1JrCJSCe-NtwESMlnG5iOgTZtwi5TANSRJcfP9UD7bRzCFa44nwSrlWcnWI4vNp1Qw3Hkvu1B40V8tzIHP6ETW96kvw-_xY1k4f5aYCN7vzN9HRro-L9FA/s1600/stns_img_0.jpg)

Diary: [7 Oct 2022](https://irwin34.blogspot.com/2022/10/day-51-108-stanchions-re-install-wind.html)

And here's what I found: at the factory they drilled the holes about an inch too far from the hull edge, so the pipes missed the wood blocks and stood "in the air". To hide it, they laid nice laminate over the gap, but didn't fill it; they just stuck in some cloth that wasn't even wet out. That mistake also left a 1/4-inch gap between the flange and the deck that the sealant had to fill: another failure point. The previous owner tried brackets to the toe rail, which only loaded the wood screws until they tore out, and lots of 5200, which can't help when the stanchion isn't solid. The blocks were rotten, and nothing was holding them.

Now came the finish: sand, open the holes and drill them clean to 1 inch, as deep as the stanchion pipe. Then a small drain hole all the way through, so if the sealant ever fails, water drips into the cabin where I'll see it. Insert the stanchions, mark the flange screws, take them out, drill and tap 2 inches deep.

Before that, I'd filled the stanchions with polyester resin, as an Irwin owner suggested. I like the idea, but resin shrinks and doesn't seal 100% to the pipe. I tested them by sucking air through them with my mouth: two of six leaked a little. So I also put 1-1/8 rubber plugs in the bottom of the pipes. That was a challenge. The best way: push the plug in as far as it goes, put a hose clamp around the part sticking out to squeeze it smaller, then drive it in with a hammer, using a bolt as a pusher.

Finally everything was cleaned with alcohol and dried, and the stanchions were set on 3M 4200 (removable, and more flexible than 5200) with 2-inch 1/4-20 machine screws.

## Lifelines from Dyneema

With the lake drying up, I wasn't rushing the lifelines. But that week friends were coming with two kids about 7 years old, so lifelines suddenly became essential, even though anyone could just swim to shore. The lake is under 3,000 feet across at its widest.

I used 1/4-inch Samson AmSteel Dyneema, top line only for now. I measured 27 feet a side, plus 3 feet for splices, times two... six extra feet should be plenty, right? WRONG. Each side has four splices, not two. Why? The gate. I figured that out right when I went to make the last splice. OK, I'll buy more along with the rope for the lower line.

My first Brummel eye splice: not bad. For the gate: a thimble on one side and the original pelican hook. I ground and polished the hook eye to be easy on the rope. ## Leaks, one by one

My November 2022 vacation confirmed the main rule: everything on deck I haven't touched is leaking. New deck handles went on, bolted from below and bedded in butyl. A two-hour water test showed no problems. All but one place got 3/8 bolts. On the long port handle, one insert was loose in the wood, so the second bolt became a big self-tapper, about 1-1/2 inch into the wood, between two bolted spots. A compromise, but it should last for years.
