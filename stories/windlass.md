@s540
After a lot of thinking I decided to bolt the windlass mount to the chain locker. My reasoning: the load can't exceed the windlass's max pull times a safety factor, say 2. Pull is 300 kg, so under 600 kg on the mount. Six 5/16 bolts would be total overkill, but the locker is only 5–6 mm fiberglass, so the load has to be spread. The bolts only stop the mount sliding up. The sideways load is taken by the locker's wedge shape. But the chain comes in about 4 inches above the mount, so it tries to lift the back of the plate, maybe up to half the main force. With two bolts per side, no bolt sees more than 300 kg of shear. And fiberglass laminate is good for 1,000–2,500 MPa; a 5/16 bolt hole in 5 mm gives about 0.00004 m², so even at 1,000 MPa that's about 40 kN, about 4 metric tons per bolt. We should be good.
So I used the chain locker itself as a mold for the mounting plates, so they match the walls exactly and spread the load evenly. Masking tape, PVA mold release, then two layers of 1708 on the walls and all the way up to the top of the mount. In real life only the corners show. The bottom view during the first layup. Then out of the "mold": now I have a perfectly matched base to build on. The cutout on one side is for the black water tank vent.

## A deeper chain locker
At first I planned 60 feet of chain and the rest rope. But when I went shopping, 90 feet (the maker's full bucket) was actually cheaper than 60 feet cut to order. So 90 feet: now I can be on all-chain rode in up to 10 feet of water, which sounds cool.
But a windlass needs at least 10 inches of free drop under the gypsy, and my locker was maybe 12 inches deep. Not enough. And I'm a lazy guy: I'd rather spend a week building a deep box than toss chain around by hand every time. Two options.
Option 1: cut through the existing locker, build a watertight bulkhead and use the whole bow as a chain locker. Pros: a really big locker, the chain kept low, and a "crash" compartment if I hit something and hole the bow. Cons: it can't drain by itself, so another pump or a drain into the main bilge, which I don't like. It's big, 30–50 gallons or more, and a wave over the bow could fill it in seconds, especially if the lid ever opened. And I'd want to reinforce the hull sides there: 6–8 mm of extra glass, laid almost upside down in a tight space. Plus that makes a hard spot in the hull, which can bring its own problems.
Option 2: cut a hole and build a 6–8 gallon bucket big enough for all the chain. Pros: it can be removable for access to the bow, a small volume can't hold much water even when full, it's much easier to build, it gets a nice inside surface if built on a mold, the hull isn't compromised, and the V-berth bow door stays. Cons: it's smaller, so less chain, the weight is higher, it's hard to measure a box that fits well, and the old drain hole must be closed and a new thru-hull made.
The decision: option 2, a self-draining box hanging from the deck, and as removable as possible, or at least easy to cut out. And glass the existing wood bulkhead watertight up to the waterline, with the door staying, mostly for some protection if the bow gets holed.

## The bow roller
Installing the bow roller at 100 °F was a challenge by itself. The main problem: I had to disconnect the forestay to drill and tap through the bow plate. The roller has two screws at the back and one at the front. The front one only takes side load and there's no way to get a nut under it, so I tapped the bow plate and screwed into it. The back screws go through the deck with nuts and big washers underneath. I added a stainless plate as thick as the bow plate between the deck and the roller, because the front sits on the bow plate and the back on fiberglass.
I'd like a backing plate, but there are two reasons I used wide washers. The surface underneath isn't flat; there's a bump in the middle that would make a stress point and put all the load on a small area. And the windlass can only pull 600 lb, so the loaded area doesn't need to be big. I'm still thinking about a wood block and a couple of bolts at the very back holes, after painting. The chain runs through the roller well, and the CQR fits.

## Its own battery
I decided the windlass gets its own battery. Why?
- I had a nice 55 Ah AGM battery.
- 15–20 feet shorter cables between the battery and the windlass.
- Even if I drain it completely, I can still start the engine and have power for the house.
- I can always recharge it from other sources.
- A spare battery on board is always nice.
So: battery, a 50 A breaker on the positive, a shunt for the battery monitor on the negative, and a 4-pole solenoid relay, controlled by a 2-channel 433 MHz wireless remote module. To "marinize" it I coated the board with polyurethane conformal coating and put it in a sealed box. The controls and solenoid on their mounting block, tested before install, then installed, with the back plate glued to the hull with epoxy and 404. The battery goes under the V-berth on the starboard side. The breaker will be inside the cabinet, doubling as a battery switch. A 12 AWG charger cable runs to the battery with its own fuse at the battery. A temporary "dry fit" electrical check.
Same day I put a class-T 300 A fuse on the lithium battery bank.

## A rope-to-chain splice
September 2026 the anchor rode. There are lots of double-braid anchor ropes, but how do people splice them to chain so it goes through the windlass? A normal eye splice makes the joint too big and stiff for the gypsy. My solution: unbraid the core and cover (keeping the strands intact), rebraid them as an 8-strand rope (the core is 8-strand anyway), then do an 8-strand rope-to-chain splice. Maybe temporary, but it doesn't look bad.

## Cracked shackles
November 2026. After four years I decided to replace the windlass motor with a brand new one. Mine sometimes lost power on long, hard pulls. I know why: when I repaired it I couldn't find the right brush springs and used what I had, and probably one spring loses strength when the brush gets hot. I'll replace the springs and keep the old motor as a spare.
During that work Vic found that both shackles on the anchor chain were cracked. When I took the pins out, I could break them in half by hand. I can't imagine how I didn't lose my anchor!!!! I replaced them with galvanized ones. There's really no point in stainless shackles between a galvanized anchor and galvanized chain.
