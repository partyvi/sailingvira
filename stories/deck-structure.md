On this boat there's one main rule: everything on the deck I haven't touched yet is leaking. I learned it the hard way, one piece of hardware at a time.

## A blob of 5200 and a wet deck
On Day 5 I finally got dock cleats for my main lines. Now I can just drop a line. The rest is still tied to the dock frame with strings.
@s116
February 2022 I opened the deck around the starboard chainplates. The hole hadn't been fixed AT ALL. The previous owner just poured a fist-sized blob of 3M 5200 and hoped it would keep the water out. No, it didn't work.
I took off the top skin. The core was partly rotten and heavily soaked. At first it didn't look too bad, but almost the whole area was spongy and wet. Four hours of grinding and sanding later, including the top skin itself. In a couple of places I cut a bit out of shape, to fix during re-coring. The last picture is the view from below.

## New core
I cut a new core from marine plywood, the same as the original. Sanded and degreased both sides, put polyester resin with a little chopped glass on the bottom skin, laid the plywood in, and loaded it with four bags of sand. I needed more, but that's all I had.
Two hours later, when the resin gelled, a layer of thickened resin went on and the old top skin went back on top, under fourteen bags of sand. Finished at 8:30 PM.
@s145
Then glassing the gaps. Easy to say, but four hours of pretty nasty work.
The non-skid pattern was a problem. It's a nice pattern, but making it again on the repaired area isn't simple. So I made a mold from the existing deck: mold release, gelcoat, one layer of 1708. I only had finishing gelcoat with me, so I didn't wait for it to fully set and laminated as soon as it gelled. It didn't gel everywhere, flaked and mixed with the resin, so it got messy. Probably I'll make it again, but it doesn't look bad even now. The original deck surface, and my home-made "negative".
@s146
Then fairing. TotalBoat fairing compound and sanding, round one. Round two. Then in April, done, with gelcoat painted on just for protection. Final fairing will happen when I paint the deck.

## Stanchions: empty boxes
Next, the stanchions. On the starboard aft one I cut the old mount out. No wood left at all. The flange had a strange 3 mm step on one side. Looks like it was meant to overlap the toe rail, but the holes were drilled in the wrong place.
I made a new oak block, drilled an oversized hole for the pipe, filled it with structural putty and drilled it again, so only plastic touches the metal. Then set it to the hull with the same putty and glassed it in: two layers of 24 oz woven roving and 1708 on top. The bottom overhang will be cut off.
@s158
Then all the old stanchion support boxes came out. All five we opened were empty, the wood rotted away completely. Someone had put a thin layer of epoxy over the cracked gelcoat and broken glass around the screws, then 3M 5200 on top. The 5200 stuck to the deck pretty well, but not at all to the stainless. And all the stanchions are hollow, so any water that gets in the pipe goes straight to the wood. Good deal for future maintenance. My dad helped me with this one.
@s162:1-4
The new blocks, ready for the structural putty. In one place somebody had "repaired" oversized holes with unthickened epoxy, and of course it fell out. I dug out all the cracks and filled the cavity with putty.
@s162:5-9
A week later: oak blocks in from inside, 1-inch holes drilled for the stanchion pipes so only plastic touches them. I marked the screw spots, drilled them out to 10 mm and filled with thickened epoxy, for 1/4-20 threads and 2-inch screws later. One layer of 3 oz glass over the stanchion holes for now, until I paint the deck. Then we glassed the blocks to the hull with two layers of 24 oz roving.

## The deck-to-hull nightmare
On the starboard side the hull-to-deck joint had no sealing at all and leaked in several places. The old sealant was dry and came out in pieces. So I decided to take the aluminum toe rail off and look.
Uff... it was hard work. Rubber mallet and oscillating tool to break the 5200. And up close, the rail was never really sealed: the sealant mostly didn't stick to the aluminum, and it covered maybe 10% of where it should.
@s180
What's under the rail? The previous owner had already "sealed" the joint: he cut a 5x5 mm groove and put some putty in it. That's it. With the hull flexing and almost no bonding area, it cracked and let water in.
So, a lot of work. I routed a slot 1/4 inch wide and 1 inch deep, 36 feet of it. Cutting that in fiberglass is no fun. Then I filled it with West System epoxy, 404 filler, microfibers and colloidal silica.
@s181
Then three layers of 4-inch 8.7 oz glass tape, on thickened epoxy to fill the low spots, and a skim of epoxy with 410 microlight on top.
@s183
New schedule: 6:30 AM to 3 PM. Too hot: 25 °C early morning and up to 40 °C in the day.
@s184
I made a stupid mistake: I left masking tape under the laminate. Then I spent ages sanding out the edge and sanded through the gelcoat where the tape was. NEVER again.
Dry fit of the rail and drilling the screw holes. I almost cried drilling holes in the thing I'd just made waterproof. Then I "painted" it all with gelcoat. Gelcoat in that heat is really hard.
Next day I sanded the gelcoat off. To get it smooth I had to sand nearly all of it away. So: no fun, time and money for nothing. Lesson: at least three coats of gelcoat, probably 3–4, or don't bother.
Then the rail went back on with 3M 4200, hoping for the best. In the heat, 4200 starts setting in 15–20 minutes, so it was a real fight to get all the screws in before it set. Two hours later it was almost cured. I still need 6–10 more screws.

## Cockpit drains from a bar sink
September 2022, one more factory shortcut. Irwin used bar sink drains as cockpit drains. Great idea, right? No. They're foil-thin brass, not made for a hose and a clamp. Mine were half eaten, and that's after a life in fresh water, with a crack spilling water into the pilot berth. I replaced them with Marelon flush-mount drains.
While I was at it I hunted for the mast ground. On the Irwin forum people said a 0-gauge wire runs from the mast base to a ground plate under the holding tank. Nonsense. Nothing there but wet plywood. That area is fully glassed in, but the sink drain hose ran through it, a perfect trap for water.
@s258
For the countersink I made my own tool: a wooden cone at the right angle, cut on my CNC, with sandpaper glued on. I tried super glue, epoxy and hot glue. Hot glue is the best, and you can change the paper easily. I worried the heat would let go of the paper, but the heat stays in a small spot and it holds fine.
@s261
Then the second drain. Just look at the original one. Foil-thin brass kitchen sink drains. Replaced with Forespar CF254 Marelon flush deck drains.
@s265
Testing showed my older son, who helped me the week before, hadn't tightened the clamps. Fixed.

## Stanchions done right
October 2022. Here's how the stanchions should be, from Irwin's own drawing. The problems with the design:
- The stanchions are hollow pipes, so water gets in easily and runs down to the wood block.
- The block has no drain, so water collects there and you don't see it until it's completely rotten.
- The pipe is welded to the flange, but not all the way around, so there are gaps.
- The flange is held to the deck with short wood screws.
@s276:1
And what I found: at the factory they drilled the holes about an inch too far from the hull edge, so the pipes missed the wood blocks and stood "in the air". To hide it they laid nice laminate over the gap, but didn't fill it, just stuck in some cloth that wasn't even wet out. That mistake also left a 1/4-inch gap between the flange and the deck that the sealant had to fill, another failure point. The previous owner tried brackets to the toe rail, which only loaded the wood screws until they tore out, and lots of 5200, which can't help when the stanchion isn't solid. Blocks rotten, nothing holding them.
@s276:2-5
Earlier I had removed all the old glass and rotten wood and made new 3x3x4-inch oak blocks, redrilled for 1-3/4 inch, set them on TotalBoat structural putty and glassed them to the hull with epoxy. Filled the hole with polyester putty and drilled 1 inch in place. Drilled the flange holes 1/2 inch and filled them with thickened epoxy. When it got too hot that summer I glassed over the holes and left them.
@s276:6-9
Now: sand, open the holes and drill them clean to 1 inch, as deep as the stanchion pipe. Then a small drain hole all the way through, so if the sealant ever fails, water drips into the cabin where I'll see it. Insert the stanchions, mark the flange screws, take them out, drill and tap 2 inches deep.
Before that I'd filled the stanchions with polyester resin, as an Irwin owner suggested. I like the idea, but resin shrinks and doesn't seal 100% to the pipe. I tested by sucking air through them with my mouth: two of six leaked a little. So I also put 1-1/8 rubber plugs in the bottom of the pipes. That was a challenge. The best way: push the plug in as far as it goes, put a hose clamp around the part sticking out to squeeze it smaller, then drive it in with a hammer and a bolt as a pusher.
Finally everything cleaned with alcohol, dried, and the stanchions set on 3M 4200 (removable, more flexible than 5200) with 2-inch 1/4-20 machine screws.

## Lifelines from Dyneema
With the lake drying up I wasn't rushing the lifelines. But that week friends were coming with two kids about 7 years old, so lifelines suddenly became essential, even though anyone could just swim to shore. The lake is under 3,000 feet across at the widest.
I used 1/4-inch Samson AmSteel Dyneema, top line only for now. I measured 27 feet a side, plus 3 feet for splices, times two... six extra feet should be plenty, right? WRONG. Each side has four splices, not two. Why? The gate. I figured that out right when I went to make the last splice. OK, I'll buy more with the rope for the lower line.
My first Brummel eye splice, not bad. For the gate: a thimble on one side and the original pelican hook. I ground and polished the hook eye to be easy on the rope. The radius is too small (AmSteel wants 3/4 inch minimum), but the rope is rated 7,400 lb, way stronger than the stanchions, so I'll leave it for now.
