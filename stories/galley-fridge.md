Then I charged it... and nothing got cold. The compressor ran and the pressure dropped almost to zero. Something was blocked. I cut the thin capillary tube and saw pressure pushing oil back from the evaporator, but nothing coming from the high side. ## Painting the cabinets
After the first coat of primer I saw the problem: plywood REALLY needs to be sealed before you paint it. So I sanded off that first coat and put on three coats of wood sealer, so the grain almost doesn't show. A lot of time, but after three coats it looks very impressive.
@s406
I felt like a squirrel in a wheel of priming and sanding. Five coats of primer in total. Sounds crazy? It is. First, Pre-Kote (the lesson: seal plywood first if you're going to paint it), and I sanded almost all of that off. Second and third, Interlux wood sealer, with sanding between. Sand again. Fourth and fifth, Pre-Kote again, sanding between. At 6 hours minimum drying per coat, that's a lot of time. And all of it in Texas summer, 40 °C outside. The AC ran 100% of the time and kept 26–28 °C inside: nice for relaxing, a bit sweaty for working.
Nice and smooth, no wood grain. Still practicing roll-and-tip. I think my brush isn't soft enough. Final paint in October.

## A hood for the condenser
Since the first day I've been thinking about making the refrigerator more efficient. The evaporator is in the ice box, the compressor under the LPG locker, and the condenser between the LPG locker and the starboard hull. A pretty "cozy" space with not much airflow, especially when it's working hard to cool 20–30 lb of stuff.
First I hung a temporary fan between the exhaust hose and the locker wall. A week later I made a hood to guide the air and mounted a fan to pull air through the condenser. The fan runs whenever the compressor runs and dumps the heat into the hull, the LPG locker and the cockpit locker. After that, the condenser output came out barely warm. Before it was 70–80 °C.

## The household compressor dies at sea
Four years ago I put the guts of a household refrigerator into my icebox. It worked surprisingly well for years of lake sailing. A few days in Galveston Bay finished it off.
A household compressor has the motor and pump hanging inside the case on springs. At sea, especially in "washing machine" conditions (2-foot waves at a 3-second period), it got thrown around so much that one hot wire rubbed through its insulation and shorted to the case. The next time it got wet, the compressor died.
I always knew it wouldn't last forever on a boat. It was a 120 V compressor too, and the long-term plan was always a proper 12 V marine/RV compressor. Well, the time came.
Brazing on a fiberglass boat isn't the safest job, so this time I took lots of precautions: an extinguisher next to me, my son standing by with a water hose, a carbon felt welding blanket over the area, and a small flame deflector that keeps the torch heat on the tube instead of everywhere. The first time I had no deflector and only wet rags. Looking back, I was playing close to the edge. This time the brazing took about five minutes, calm and much more professional.
Then a 20-minute vacuum and a charge of exactly 1.8 oz of R-134a. Everything went as planned.
The box was loaded with about 20 bottles of beer and cider, so it took a while. After about four hours of hard work the compressor started cycling, holding 5 °C inside. All that with the compressor limited to 60 W. It's a BLDC motor and can run much faster, but I'm not sure there's any real benefit. Next time I'll let everything settle and measure the average power.
@s902
August 2026 the compressor stopped switching on. After three years, the little relay contacts inside the cheap thermostat had failed. Probably because it drives the coil of a 30 A relay that has no flyback diode. Thermostat replaced. I still need to add the diode.

## So I built my own thermostat
I went through three of those cheap Chinese thermostats in four years. I think the problem is the sensor and its circuit: moisture and vibration make them unreliable. The last one, after a few days, showed +26 °C when the box was cold and +2 °C when it was warm. Maybe interference from the AC wiring, but I got tired of this battle.
Welcome to my life. I designed my own: a 1-Wire digital temperature sensor, remote control, a link to the boat computer, a food safety alarm if it stays above 7 °C too long, and extra compressor protection. It's built around an ESP-12F module, a great fit for this, on a board made for a small IP68 box. Full user manuals are on the blog, and schematics, BOM and Gerbers are available on request.
@s941:1-6
What it does: it holds the box temperature, runs the fan in the box if there is one, and can drive a variable-speed DC compressor to boost cooling when needed. With extra sensors it can watch a freezer, the compressor, the condenser and the evaporator and tell you when something's wrong. Any alarm can be turned off.
The main interface is as simple as it gets: one button with an RGB light in it, and a buzzer. On and off, mute alarms, and read the alarm code when there's a problem. Most of the time the light will just be blue. It has day and night modes, so it doesn't blind you at night.
@s941:7-8
Everything else is in the web page over Wi-Fi: set the temperature, assign sensors, set alarm temperatures, turn alarms on or off, compressor settings, and firmware updates, in Basic and Advanced modes. MQTT for OpenPlotter, and a serial port for a "nice" display, though I think that's overkill. The alarm codes:
- Minor, keeps cooling (red flash every 5 s): 11 door open, 22 cooler below 0 °C, 24 freezer below −25 °C, 25 extra sensor lost, 26 cooler above 10 °C, 27 freezer above −5 °C, 31 compressor hot, 32 condenser too hot, 35 evaporator below −30 °C.
- Food safety, keeps cooling (amber): 21 cooler warm for 30 minutes, 23 freezer warm for 30 minutes. Check the food, then double-click.
- Major, cooling stopped (steady red): 51 not cooling, 52 cooler sensor lost, 53 compressor overheated. Fix it, then power cycle.

## A real lid
The refrigerator lid had zero insulation. It was just a plug for the hole in the countertop, and for four years after the conversion it was a piece of foam. I wanted better insulation, and a lid that closes airtight so humid air doesn't build up ice on the evaporator.
I modeled two parts in 3D. An outer frame, glued into the opening, sealed around the insulation and epoxied to the walls so the top insulation can't get wet. And an inner shell, nested inside the frame, that becomes the lid: filled with foam and glassed over so the foam can't soak up water. A gasket goes between them.
I printed the parts only 0.8 mm thick. They're just shapes to glass over. The frame needed trimming in place, because the boat is far from square and parallel. The lid shell got one layer of CSM on the bottom and inside walls, and the frame got glassed on the outside.
@s944
Between boat days I painted the frame and lid with topside paint to make them white and seal the printed parts. Then I sealed the fridge foam with epoxy and installed the frame, and the lid was assembled.
